title: "Drusen: what they are, and when they can clear"
---

<p>By Dr. Glen Swartwout, Naturopathic Optometrist</p>
<p>I am retired from licensed practice, and I now write and teach about natural eye care.</p>
<p>This page is education, not a diagnosis or a treatment plan.</p>
<p>Drusen are small yellow deposits that build up under the retina.</p>
<p>Small hard drusen are common with age and often harmless.</p>
<p>Larger soft drusen are a sign of macular degeneration and of higher risk.</p>
<p>Many people are told drusen only grow.</p>
<p>The research says something more hopeful, and more careful.</p>
<h2>What drusen are made of</h2>
<p>The retina's support layer is called the retinal pigment epithelium.</p>
<p>It sends fats out toward the blood supply behind the eye.</p>
<p>With age, the membrane it sends them through thickens and the fats back up.</p>
<p>One researcher describes a "major ultrastructural component" of soft drusen as cholesterol-rich lipoproteins from that layer.</p>
<p>Source: <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30357336/">Soft Drusen in Age-Related Macular Degeneration: Biology and Targeting Via the Oil Spill Strategies</a></p>
<p>She calls macular degeneration "a vascular-metabolic-inflammatory disease."</p>
<p>Source: <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30357336/">Soft Drusen in Age-Related Macular Degeneration: Biology and Targeting Via the Oil Spill Strategies</a></p>
<p>Another team proposed that cell debris seeds a deposit and inflammation then builds around it.</p>
<p>Source: <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12208254/">A role for local inflammation in the formation of drusen in the aging eye</a></p>
<h2>Drusen can shrink</h2>
<p>Eye scans that measure drusen over time show they do not only grow.</p>
<p>One study measured drusen volume over a year.</p>
<p>It "increased in 48% of eyes, remained stable in 40%, and decreased in 12%".</p>
<p>Source: <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21724264/">Natural history of drusen morphology in age-related macular degeneration using spectral domain optical coherence tomography</a></p>
<p>Over two years, another study found some regression in about half of the eyes it followed.</p>
<p>Those areas often showed changes on a second kind of scan, called autofluorescence.</p>
<p>Source: <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23830564/">Drusen regression is associated with local changes in fundus autofluorescence in intermediate age-related macular degeneration</a></p>
<h2>Shrinking is not always healing</h2>
<p>Drusen sometimes shrink just before macular degeneration reaches its late stage.</p>
<p>In one study, drusen regressed in 22 of 50 eyes.</p>
<p>Four of those eyes then grew leaky new vessels, and three developed atrophy.</p>
<p>The authors concluded: "Spontaneous regression appears to precede conversion to advanced AMD."</p>
<p>Source: <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27044341/">Drusen volume development over time and its relevance to the course of age-related macular degeneration</a></p>
<p>So a smaller deposit on its own proves nothing.</p>
<p>In my view, healing looks different.</p>
<p>The drusen shrink and the retina over them stays intact.</p>
<p>Vision holds or improves.</p>
<p>Studies show drusen can shrink without atrophy.</p>
<p>These studies measured the scans and retinal function, not whether vision improved.</p>
<p>A 2025 study found regression without atrophy in "24.17% of the eyes" it followed with soft drusen.</p>
<p>Most of those regressions sat next to another druse that did collapse with atrophy.</p>
<p>Source: <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40024370/">Spontaneous Soft Drusen Regression without Atrophy and the Drusen Ooze</a></p>
<p>A study of 14 people found soft drusen can fade with no detectable local loss of function.</p>
<p>Their retinas were still less sensitive overall than those of healthy people.</p>
<p>The authors warn that this fading is "a potential source of misclassification."</p>
<p>Source: <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19535356/">Functional aspects of drusen regression in age-related macular degeneration</a></p>
<p>True clearing can only be shown by scans and vision tests over time.</p>
<h2>What research has tried</h2>
<p>Very few treatment studies have measured drusen directly.</p>
<p>In a small high-dose statin study, 23 people completed a year.</p>
<p>Ten of them had drusen regress.</p>
<p>The study had no comparison group.</p>
<p>The authors called for "Confirmation from larger studies."</p>
<p>Source: <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27077128/">Regression of Some High-risk Features of Age-related Macular Degeneration (AMD) in Patients Receiving Intensive Statin Treatment</a></p>
<p>A study of 3,791 people found statin use was not linked with slower progression to late disease.</p>
<p>Source: <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26435335/">The Association of Statin Use with Age-Related Macular Degeneration Progression: The Age-Related Eye Disease Study 2 Report Number 9</a></p>
<p>Laser can reduce drusen.</p>
<p>It did not reduce the risk of leaky new vessels.</p>
<p>It was not shown to limit atrophy or vision loss.</p>
<p>Source: <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26493180/">Laser treatment of drusen to prevent progression to advanced age-related macular degeneration</a></p>
<p>People who ate closer to a Mediterranean diet were less likely to progress to large drusen or late disease.</p>
<p>In one of the two groups, the benefit appeared mainly in people with certain protective genes.</p>
<p>That study did not measure regression.</p>
<p>Source: <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32348832/">Adherence to the Mediterranean Diet and Progression to Late Age-Related Macular Degeneration in the Age-Related Eye Disease Studies 1 and 2</a></p>
<h2>How I think about drusen</h2>
<p>What follows is my own view, from many years in natural eye care.</p>
<p>It is not proven by the studies above.</p>
<p>I see drusen as a waste problem, where the eye takes in more than it can clear.</p>
<p>Researchers have compared the inflammation in drusen to that in Alzheimer's disease and atherosclerosis.</p>
<p>Source: <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12208254/">A role for local inflammation in the formation of drusen in the aging eye</a></p>
<p>I see a parallel there too.</p>
<p>I believe sugar damage to proteins plays a large part.</p>
<p>That damage is called glycation.</p>
<p>I have nicknamed macular degeneration "diabetes type four" to make that point.</p>
<p>It does not mean macular degeneration is a form of diabetes.</p>
<p>I also believe the liver matters, because it processes fats.</p>
<p>Circulation matters too, because the retina uses a great deal of oxygen.</p>
<p>So the approaches I teach aim to address four things.</p>
<ul>
<li>Glycation, through diet and blood sugar balance.</li>
<li>The liver's handling of fats.</li>
<li>Circulation and oxygen to the back of the eye.</li>
<li>Good nutrition for the retina.</li>
</ul>
<p>None of these four approaches has been shown in a trial to clear drusen.</p>
<h2>What to ask your eye doctor</h2>
<p>If you have drusen, the only way to know what is happening is to measure it.</p>
<p>Ask for an OCT scan that measures drusen volume, and keep the reports.</p>
<p>Ask whether the retina over any shrinking drusen is intact.</p>
<p>Track your vision with the same chart each time.</p>
<p>Repeat the scans on the same machine at regular intervals.</p>
<p>Keep working with your own eye doctor throughout.</p>
